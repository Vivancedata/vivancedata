*Written in January 2025 and rewritten since. The original carried a two-year forecast and a run of examples from banking, hospitals and warehouse dashboards; both are gone. Predictions at that range have a poor record, mine included, and none of it helped anybody decide what to do next month. What is left is the part that held up, which is mostly about how to read the claims.*

Your project software has AI in it now. So does your accounting package, apparently. There's a fold-out in the trade magazine, three emails from suppliers you've never bought from, and a whole aisle of it at the show. Some of that is a genuine change in what these things can do. A good deal of it is a rename of a feature that shipped four years ago.

You do not have time to sort that out properly, and nobody selling to you has any interest in helping you. So here is how I'd read it, from someone who builds this stuff and would still rather you didn't buy any of it than bought the wrong thing.

## Distrust the numbers, including the impressive ones

You'll be shown figures about how many businesses are using this and how much time it saves them. Almost all of them are worth nothing to you, for two reasons.

The first is what's being counted. "Uses AI in at least one part of the business" counts a firm where somebody once had a chatbot write a job advert exactly the same as a firm that has rebuilt how work gets dispatched. The survey measures the first and gets quoted as evidence of the second.

The second is who's doing the measuring. Time-saved figures are usually reported by the people who chose the tool, measured on the tasks the tool is good at, and hardly ever netted against the time spent checking its output. That's a real saving for that person on that task, and it tells you nothing about what your whole week looks like afterwards.

The move that actually works is to stop reading other people's numbers and generate one of your own. Count one job for a fortnight before you change anything — how many after-hours calls, how long the slips take, who does them. That single number is worth more than every statistic you'll be shown, because it's yours.

## Distrust the urgency, including when I use it

The claim that a window is closing is the oldest device in technology sales. It exists to shorten your evaluation, not to inform it. There's no version of this where a business that waited six months and picked well ends up behind one that moved in six weeks and picked badly.

You are not late. The firms getting something out of this are not the fast ones; they're the ones that picked a job that was genuinely costing them and checked afterwards whether it got better.

## What has genuinely changed, and why it matters to you specifically

Three things are different from a few years ago, and they happen to be different in ways that matter more in your trade than in most.

**It handles input that isn't tidy.** This is the change. Older systems needed the clean version: the typed form, the caller who spoke clearly. Yours is never the clean version. It's a slip photographed at an angle in a cab, a voicemail with a compressor running behind it, a note written on the back of a card with a job number that has a letter in it that could be a 1. Software that only works on tidy input was never any use to you, which is why the last twenty years of it mostly wasn't. That's the bit worth paying attention to.

**Some of it now does a sequence rather than answering a question.** Not just "what does this document say" but read it, check it against the order, flag the discrepancy, put it in front of a person. That's more useful and considerably more dangerous, for one reason: a system that acts on something untrue acts on it fast. Give it a stale van stock list and it will order parts confidently, wrongly, and quicker than a person would have. The interesting question about any of these is never what it can do. It's what it's allowed to do without asking, and who decided that.

**Narrow usually beats general on your material.** A system set up on your documents, your job numbers, your customers' names, will normally do better on your work than a general-purpose one. That's not a marketing claim, it's just what happens when you stop making something guess. And it's cheap to test — try both on twenty of your own real documents and you'll know in an afternoon.

## The hard part is never the clever part

Here is the thing I'd most want an owner to know before signing anything.

Reading the delivery slip is the easy half. Getting the numbers out of it and into the system you already run — in the format that system wants, without breaking whatever else writes to it, in a way that still works when the supplier changes their form — is where the hours are. The intelligence has become the cheap part. The plumbing has not, and never will.

So when you're being sold something, the question that separates the real from the demonstration is: what does it connect to, and who does that work? If the answer is vague, you're being quoted for half a job.

## Buy the standard thing where a standard thing exists

Most of what a small trade business needs already exists and can be bought. A dispatch system. A decent job management package. An answering service staffed by people. If a product covers most of what you need and the gap isn't the part that makes you different from the firm down the road, buy it. It'll be cheaper, it'll be maintained by somebody else, and it'll still be there in five years.

Build only where you're genuinely odd — the process nobody sells for, the documents nobody else's customers produce, the thing you do differently that's the reason people use you. That's a narrow set, and it should be. I'd rather scope one workflow for someone than sell them a platform, and if the honest answer on your call is "buy the off-the-shelf one", that's what you'll hear.

And sometimes the answer isn't software at all. If your two weeks of counting turns up four after-hours calls a month and three are the same customer asking about an invoice, the fix is a phone message and a conversation, not a system.

## The bits everyone skips

Your people will have the job-security conversation whether or not you're in it. Have it early, be specific about what changes for them, and involve whoever does the work in the design, because they know where the exceptions are and the exceptions are what break these things.

Your records set the ceiling. Nothing above them can be better than what's in them.

And something you connect to your data is a new door into your business. It doesn't need to be a project, but it needs to be a decision somebody made.

## The question worth sitting with

Not how fast can you adopt AI. That question has no useful answer and it's the one the sales material wants you asking.

The two that matter: which single job in your week would you point this at first, and how would you know afterwards whether it worked? If you can't answer the second one, the first is premature, and the fortnight of counting is the whole job.
