You don't need a business case. There's no committee and no finance director. It's your money, and the question is whether this pays for itself or becomes another monthly bill you resent by March.

You can get most of the way to an answer on the back of an envelope. Below is the math, the costs that never show up on a quote, and three ways the math flatters itself.

## Start with a count

Everything here depends on a number you wrote down before anything changed: how many after-hours calls, how many slips a week, how long each takes. If you don't have it yet, [spend two weeks getting it](/blog/count-it-first). The rest of this post assumes you did.

## The benefit side

Three things go here, in the order I'd trust them.

Hours, at what an hour costs you. Use the loaded number: wage plus payroll taxes plus that person's share of overhead. Your accountant has it, and it's noticeably bigger than what's on the pay stub. Multiply by the hours the job takes each week and the weeks you work. There's a catch on this line, covered in the next section.

Work you're losing. People leave this out, and in the trades it's often bigger than the hours. If after-hours calls go to voicemail, some of those callers book someone else before you call back. You know the call count from your two weeks. You know what an average job is worth and roughly what share of inquiries you close. Multiply the three. It's a soft number, since you can't know how many of those callers would have picked you, so write it as a range with the assumption next to it. [The missed-call calculator](/tools/missed-calls) does this multiplication if you'd like it done for you.

Mistakes that cost money. The two pallets nobody noticed until the customer called. The return trip because the wrong part was on the van. The submittal that went out with last month's revision. Put your cost on one of those and multiply by how often it happens.

Below all three sits risk: the fine you didn't get, the claim that never came. Note it and don't build the case on it. You'd be counting things that didn't happen.

## The catch on the hours line

Say the count shows data entry takes one morning a week. That morning only turns into money if one of three things is true.

You skip a hire you were about to make. You cut hours you were paying for. Or the morning goes to work that brings money in, like quoting the jobs sitting in the inbox or chasing unpaid invoices.

If none of those is true, the morning gets absorbed. The work is more pleasant, your office manager is less fed up, and the P&L doesn't move. That has value, because people do quit over paperwork. Just keep it out of the money column, or you'll wonder later where the money went.

Decide which of the three you're claiming. In a small shop it's usually the third. It holds up as long as you can name the work that fills the gap.

## The cost side, including what nobody quotes

You'll be quoted for the software and the build. Three more costs sit under that, and together they often come to more than the quote.

Connecting it to what you already run. Reading a delivery slip is the easy half. Getting the numbers into your system in the shape it wants is where the hours go. Check that the quote covers both.

The changeover. A few weeks of running both ways, someone learning it, a stretch where things take longer than before. If your math assumes full benefit from week one, it's off by however long that takes.

Keeping it alive. Supplier forms change and so does the software underneath. Budget a monthly amount for someone to watch it. I explain [what that fee pays for](/blog/what-the-monthly-fee-covers) in another post. A quote where the cost drops to zero after the build describes a system nobody is looking at.

Then add your own time. Nobody invoices you for it, but you spend it.

## Three ways the math flatters itself

It handles the easy ones and leaves you the hard ones. Automation rarely removes a whole job. It removes the simple cases. If the clean slips go through and the water-stained, half-legible ones still come to a person, the average time on what's left goes up. The headline hours look great while the person doing the leftovers has a worse week than before. Estimate the work that remains, too.

Some of the improvement would have happened anyway. You also hired someone, or the season turned, or the supplier finally started sending PDFs instead of faxes. The new system gets the credit because it's the change everyone can see.

The same win gets counted twice. Faster handling and fewer hours get added together when one caused the other. So do "errors avoided" and "rework avoided" when it was one error.

## Numbers that move when nothing improved

Be suspicious of any measure you can improve by changing how you behave.

"Calls handled without a person" counts the caller who gave up the same as the one who got help. Pair it with whether those callers called back.

Time per document drops when the system works. It also drops when the hard documents get routed to someone else.

Usage tells you people opened it. If you told everyone they have to use it, usage will look excellent.

For each number you plan to watch, pick a second one that would catch the first one lying.

## The figure that decides it

Look at break-even before you look at the return.

Take your biggest assumption, such as how many of those after-hours callers you'd have won, and ask how far it can fall before the thing stops paying for itself. If it only works when every assumption lands at the good end, that's worth knowing for the price of an afternoon.

Then compare against what else the money could do. A monthly bill is a monthly bill whether it goes to software or payroll. Set this against a part-time person in the office, a second van, or an apprentice. Sometimes the apprentice wins.

## Write down what would make you stop

"If I'm still checking every summary by hand in three months, this isn't working." "If it hasn't caught a short delivery by the end of the quarter, I stop paying for it."

Something that can only ever be called a success is something you'll still be paying for in five years out of embarrassment. Writing the stopping rule now, while you're calm, costs nothing.

## Keep the envelope

When real numbers come in, put them next to your prediction and leave the prediction alone.

A result far better than you projected deserves as much suspicion as a bad one. It usually means an assumption was off. If you quietly revise the prediction as results arrive, you lose the ability to tell.
