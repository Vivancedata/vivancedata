# AI Implementation Guide: From Strategy to Production

Most AI projects that go wrong don't go wrong on the technology. They go wrong because nobody wrote down which problem was being solved, or because the plan quietly assumed people would work differently on the morning the system went live. The model is rarely the part that decides it.

What follows is a sequence for getting from an idea to something running in production. The week numbers are a shape rather than a promise: a document-processing job inside one office moves faster than anything that has to touch a dispatch board three departments care about.

## Phase 1: strategic foundation (weeks 1-2)

### Write the problem down before you look at tools

Four answers, on one page, before anyone demos anything.

The problem, stated so that the person who lives with it would recognise the description. Not "improve efficiency" — the invoice typed into two systems by two different people, or the 9pm no-heat call that goes to voicemail and then to whoever answers first by morning.

How you will know it got better. Pick the measure now, while you can still take a reading of what it is today. Afterwards is too late; that reading doesn't exist any more.

The timeline, judged against who is actually free to work on this, not against the vendor's slide.

The budget, which is never only the software. Training, integration, and the monthly cost of keeping the thing alive after it ships all belong in the number.

### Assess readiness honestly

On data: is there enough of it, can somebody actually get at it, and does any of it carry privacy or compliance weight?

On systems: can what you already run be connected to, do you have the compute, and does your security setup survive a new integration?

On appetite: is there someone senior who wants this to happen, is there any tolerance for the change it implies, and is there one person inside who will champion it day to day? A project with no champion becomes nobody's problem the first week it misbehaves.

On skills: what exists internally, what's missing, and can you hire it or must you teach it?

### Pick the first use case for its ability to finish

The first project should be chosen for whether it can be completed, not for how impressive it sounds. You want an outcome you can see, a result inside a few months, no requirement that the whole company reorganise, and enough value that finishing it matters.

Good first jobs tend to look like document processing, sorting incoming customer messages, forecasting from numbers you already keep, or drafting marketing copy someone then edits.

Bad first jobs are the ones where being wrong is expensive: anything mission-critical, anything with many moving parts, anything a regulator takes an interest in, and anything that needs a model built from scratch.

I would rather ship a narrow thing that works than pilot a broad thing that generates meetings.

## Phase 2: planning and design (weeks 3-6)

### Who you need

Someone senior who owns the budget and can say yes or no without convening anything. Someone keeping the schedule honest. Someone who understands the actual work — the person who does the job today, not the person who manages them. Someone technical to build and run the AI part. Someone who knows the systems it has to plug into. And someone whose job is the human side: telling people what is changing, and why, before it changes.

In a small business that is three people wearing six hats, which is fine. What is not fine is nobody holding the last one.

### Decide the architecture

Build, buy, or configure. Off-the-shelf software is fastest and bends least. A configurable platform sits in the middle. Custom development gives you the most control, takes the longest, and commits you to maintaining it for as long as you use it.

Cloud or on-premise. Cloud scales, deploys quickly, and bills you every month. On-premise keeps the data in the building and costs more up front.

How it connects. An API integration is clean and maintainable and takes real development time. A native integration is quicker and less flexible. A batch pipeline is the right answer whenever the work does not need answering in the moment.

### Write the plan down

Milestones and what each one produces. Who is on it, for how many hours a week. What could go wrong and what you would do about it. Who gets told what, and when. What training is needed. And how you will test the thing before anyone depends on it.

## Phase 3: development and testing (weeks 7-14)

### Data preparation

Budget for this to consume more of the schedule than the modelling does. It routinely does, and it is the phase most often left out of the plan.

The work is gathering the sources, cleaning out errors and deciding what to do about the gaps, labelling if your approach needs labels, reshaping everything into a form the model can read, and then checking the result is what you think it is.

Don't aim for perfect data. Get to good enough, ship, and improve it against what the system actually gets wrong rather than against what you imagined it would.

### Model development or configuration

If you are building: start with the simplest model that could plausibly work, keep a held-out test set from day one, and write down every experiment including the ones that failed. A baseline you can beat is worth more than a clever result you cannot explain.

If you are configuring something you bought: set it up for your case, write the prompts and rules against your own material, get authentication and permissions right early, and test it on real inputs. The messy ones — the handwritten delivery note, the PDF that is really a photograph — not the tidy sample the vendor supplied.

### Integration

Four questions decide whether this survives contact with a normal Tuesday.

What happens when the AI is wrong? What happens when it is unavailable? How does a person take over, and do they know they need to? And what do you log — would the log tell you why something went sideways three weeks later, when the person who built it is on holiday?

### Testing

Test that it does what it was designed to do. Test that it holds up at the volume you actually get rather than the volume you demoed. Test that it is accurate enough, deciding what "enough" means before you look at the result rather than after. Put it in front of the people who will use it and watch what they do, which is not always what they say. Test it for security holes. And check whether it behaves differently for different groups of people, because if it does you want to be the one who finds out.

## Phase 4: pilot (weeks 15-20)

### Run it beside the old way

Pick a small group, 10-50 people, and pick the ones who will tell you when it is annoying rather than the ones who will be polite. Keep the existing process running alongside. Gather numbers and complaints in equal measure, and be ready to change things quickly — a pilot nobody is allowed to change is a demo with witnesses.

### Watch these while it runs

Whether people are using it or quietly going around it. How often it is right. Whether it is fast enough that using it beats not using it. What the people using it say when nobody senior is in the room. And whether anything moved in the business itself: time, cost, quality.

### Expect to find these

Users need more training than you assumed. The interface needs simplifying. An integration point turns out to be wrong. Accuracy is fine in general and poor on one specific kind of input, which is usually the kind that matters most to somebody.

Don't skip this phase. What you find in a pilot costs hours. The same thing found in production costs weeks.

## Phase 5: full deployment (weeks 21-26)

### Before you scale

Write down how it works and who to call. Make training material people will actually open: a short video, a one-page guide, and an FAQ built from the questions the pilot really produced. Set up somewhere for questions to go. Get monitoring in place before you need it rather than during the first incident.

### Roll out in waves

The pilot group first, then the early adopters and champions, then the wider business, then everyone. Weeks between waves, not days.

Waves keep your support capacity from drowning and leave room to correct course. They also mean each group hears that it works from a colleague rather than from you, which lands differently.

### Training

Live sessions while it is new. Recordings for the people who miss them, and everyone misses one. Written documentation for the detail nobody remembers. A one-page reference covering the five things people do daily. Office hours at a fixed time, kept up for as long as questions keep coming. And a channel where people can ask each other, which is where most of the real answers end up.

## Phase 6: keeping it working (ongoing)

### Review cycles

Weekly, look at usage and anything on fire. Monthly, look at whether the effect you promised is showing up and at what users think. Quarterly, ask whether this is still worth what it costs — a question worth asking honestly, because sometimes the answer is no and that is a result too.

### After a few months of stable running

Add what users keep asking for. Extend to the next use case. Connect the systems you left out of scope the first time. Improve accuracy on the cases you now know it fails, which by this point you have real examples of rather than guesses about.

### Build the capability

The point of the first project is not the first project. It is the people inside who now know how to run the second one: a couple of internal champions, a habit of sharing what was learned, budget that permits an experiment to fail, and an outside relationship for the parts you would rather not own.

## Where it usually goes wrong

### People don't want it

Tell them early and be specific about what changes for them personally. Involve the people who do the work in the design, because they know where the exceptions live and the exceptions are what break these systems. Ship something small that helps somebody in the first week. And answer the job-security question directly instead of hoping it is not being asked, because it is.

### The data is worse than you thought

Start the cleanup before you need it. Decide what "clean enough" means and stop when you get there. Give one person ownership of it, and budget for keeping it clean rather than treating cleanup as a one-off.

### The scope keeps growing

Write down what is in and what is out, and make "out" an actual list rather than an absence. Have a route for new requests that is not "sure, we'll add it". Keep a phase-two backlog: it is the most useful document in the project, because it lets you say yes and no in the same sentence.

### The integration is harder than the AI

Simplify the architecture wherever you can, since every connection is something to maintain. Prefer standard APIs and protocols. Assume you will be maintaining this in two years, and write it for that person.

## Measuring success

In the first weeks you can only see leading signs: how many people are using it, whether it stays up, how many support tickets it throws off, what users say about it.

From a few months out the real ones arrive — time and cost, quality, revenue where it applies, and how all of that compares against what you said in the business case.

Some of it never becomes a number. Whether people would object if you switched it off tomorrow is worth knowing, and no dashboard reports it.

## Conclusion

Implementation is mostly people, process and change management, and only a little bit technology. Teams that know this plan for it and get somewhere. Teams that don't buy a good tool and watch it go unused.

Start from a problem you can state in one sentence. Pick a first project small enough to finish. Spend properly on the human side. Take the baseline reading before you deploy, because it cannot be recovered afterwards. Then change things based on what the first version actually taught you, not on what the plan predicted it would.

None of this is fast. It does not need to be. It needs to still be working in a year.
