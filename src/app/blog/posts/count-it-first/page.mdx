Nearly everything I write here says the same thing somewhere in the middle: count the job for two weeks first. This is the post those sentences point to.

The reason is simple. Once you change how a job gets done, the old numbers are gone. You can't go back and measure how many calls you used to miss. So if you ever want to know whether an automation paid for itself, or you want to talk yourself out of one, you need the count from before.

You don't need software for it. A clipboard by the phone works. So does a note on your phone or a sheet of paper taped inside the truck.

## Pick one job

One. If you count three things at once you'll quit by Thursday.

Pick the job you'd describe to a friend as "the thing that keeps going wrong." After-hours calls nobody returns. Delivery slips typed in by hand. Field notes matched to job numbers on Friday afternoon.

## What to write down

Keep it to tally marks and a few words. If a row takes more than ten seconds, you won't keep doing it.

For after-hours calls:

```
After-hours calls, two weeks starting <date>

Date/time  | What it was        | Called back? | Became a job?
-----------|--------------------|--------------|----------------------
Tue 9:10p  | no heat            | yes, 7:05a   | no, booked elsewhere
Tue 9:31p  | same caller again  | -            | -
Wed 6:40p  | invoice question   | yes          | n/a
```

For paperwork:

```
Delivery slips, two weeks starting <date>

- Slips received:               <count>
- Minutes to enter one:         <time three, take the middle one>
- Who entered them:             <name>
- Entered twice or corrected:   <count>
- Problems found same day:      <count>
- Problems found by a customer: <count>
```

For field notes, count the notes, the minutes spent matching them to jobs, and how many needed a call to the tech to figure out.

## Four ways the count fools you

A quiet stretch. Two weeks in a mild October tells an HVAC shop nothing about January. If the job is seasonal, say so next to the number, or count again when it's busy.

Repeat callers. One person calling three times in twenty minutes is one job. Mark repeats so they don't inflate the total.

Counting from memory. Tally at the moment it happens. A count reconstructed on Friday always comes out lower and tidier than the real week.

The person being counted knows. If the office manager knows data entry is being timed, the slips get entered faster for two weeks. Tell them why you're counting. It's about the job, and they're the one who knows where it goes wrong, so ask them to add a note whenever something odd comes through. Those notes are often worth more than the tally.

## Reading the result

Three outcomes are common.

It's smaller than you thought. Four after-hours calls a month, three of them about invoices. Change your voicemail greeting to say when the office opens and move on. You just saved yourself a project.

It's bigger than you thought, and it's one thing. Most of the lost calls are no-heat calls between 6 and 10pm. Most of the retyping comes from one supplier's slips. That's a job with edges, and it's the kind worth automating.

It's bigger than you thought, and it's five things. The "paperwork problem" turns out to be slips, plus timesheets, plus a quoting spreadsheet, plus two other things. Pick the one with the most tally marks and count that alone for another week.

## What to do with the number

Put it through the math. [The missed-call calculator](/tools/missed-calls) takes three figures from an after-hours count and gives you a rough yearly cost. [The back-of-an-envelope post](/blog/is-it-worth-automating) covers the paperwork version, including the ways the math lies.

Keep the sheet. If you do go ahead, it's your "before." Six months later you count the same things the same way and compare.
