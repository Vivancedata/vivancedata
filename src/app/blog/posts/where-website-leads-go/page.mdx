Someone finds your site on a Sunday, fills in the "request a quote" form, and hits Send. The page says thanks. Then what?

On a lot of trade websites, not much. The request exists, technically. It's in an inbox nobody opens, or a spam folder, or the dashboard of a website builder the owner last logged into two years ago. The customer waits a day, hears nothing, and calls someone else. You never know they existed.

It's the same lost job as the unanswered 9pm call, except this one doesn't even leave a voicemail.

## Five places a request goes missing

It emails an address nobody reads. The form was set up to send to info@ or to whoever built the site. That person left, or that inbox filled up with newsletters.

It lands in spam. A form email is sent by a website on behalf of a stranger, which is close to what spam looks like. Without the right sending setup, some of them get filtered. The mail provider makes that call, so the site never finds out.

You can't reply to it. The email arrives "from" your own website. Hit reply and you're writing to yourself or to a no-reply address. To answer the customer you have to copy their address out of the message, and on a phone in a truck that step waits until later.

It only lives in a dashboard. Some builders store submissions on their side and email you a notice. If the notice fails, the request sits in a list you'd have to log in to see.

Nothing tells the customer it worked. With no confirmation, they can't tell a sent request from a broken form. Some submit twice. Some assume it failed and leave.

## A ten-minute test

Do this today, from your phone, off your office Wi-Fi.

Fill in your own form the way a customer would, with a personal email address and a realistic request. Note the time. Then check four things. Did the request reach someone who'd act on it, and how long did it take? Did it arrive in the main inbox or in spam? If you hit reply, where does the reply go? Did the "customer" get anything back?

Then do it again on a Saturday night, because that's when people have time to fill in forms.

If all four answers are good, you're fine and you can stop reading.

## What a form should do

Send each request to a person right away, at an address someone checks on their phone.

Put the customer in the reply-to. You open the message, hit reply, and you're writing to the customer. That one setting decides whether you answer in two minutes or tomorrow.

Send from a properly set-up address so the message isn't treated as spam. It's a few DNS records. It's dull, and it gets skipped.

Tell the customer it worked, on the page and by email, with a plain statement of when they'll hear back. Only promise what you'll do.

Fail loudly. If the send fails, the page should say so and show a phone number or an email address. A form that says "thanks!" while the message goes nowhere is the worst version, because everyone believes it worked.

If you run Jobber, you can skip the inbox. The form can create the client and the request directly, so it shows up where you already schedule work and nobody retypes it.

## What to ask a web designer

How do form submissions reach me, and what happens if that fails?

Whose name are the domain and hosting in? It should be yours.

Can I watch a test submission arrive on my phone before I pay the final invoice?

A site that looks great and drops requests is a brochure. For a trade business, the form is the part that earns money.
