# Data Preparation for AI: The Foundation of Success

A model can only learn what its data contains, and most data contains less than the people who own it think. Preparing it routinely takes more of the schedule than the modelling does, and it is almost always the phase that was underestimated when the plan was written.

This is a practical route through that work: what to look at, what to fix, and how to know when it is fit to train on.

## Why it matters

### Where AI projects actually break

Very few projects fail on algorithm selection or compute. They fail on data.

There isn't enough of it, so the model never sees the pattern often enough to learn it. What exists is full of errors, inconsistencies and gaps. It carries the shape of past decisions, so the model reproduces them and calls it a prediction. It sits in a system nobody can get an export out of. Or it isn't labelled, and the approach you chose needs labels.

### What poor data costs

Three failure patterns are worth recognising, because each one produces a model
that trains cleanly and fails quietly.

**Missing context in the source records.** Consider a readmission prediction model
built on discharge records where the post-discharge care plan is frequently absent.
The model does not know the field is missing for a reason; it learns from the
records that happen to be complete, which are not a random sample of patients.

**Data that records the outcome of a constraint rather than the demand.** Consider
a demand forecast trained on historical sales that does not distinguish "nobody
wanted it" from "it was out of stock". The model learns to predict low demand for
exactly the items you kept failing to stock, and the forecast then justifies not
stocking them.

**Duplicate records that concentrate in one segment.** Consider a fraud model whose
training data contains duplicated entries from a single region. The duplicates
inflate the apparent frequency of whatever those records contain, and the model
becomes miscalibrated for that population specifically — which is hard to see in
an aggregate accuracy score.

## The five stages of data preparation

### Stage 1: discovery and assessment

Before you can prepare data, you need to know what you have.

Inventory the sources: internal databases like the CRM and the ERP, file storage full of spreadsheets and documents, anything bought or downloaded from outside, real-time streams from sensors or web analytics, and the unstructured pile — emails, PDFs, photographs of paperwork.

Then assess each one on five questions. What share of fields is populated? How often are the values correct? Do they match what the same field says in another system? Is the data current? And does it conform to the formats and ranges you expect?

Record it like this:

```
Customer Database Audit:
- Total records:            <count>
- Email completeness:       <populated / total>
- Email validity:           <passing format check / populated>
- Phone completeness:       <populated / total>
- Duplicate records:        <count, and the rule used to detect them>
- Recency distribution:     <share updated within N months>
```

Write the detection rule down next to each figure. "Duplicates: 3,200" means
nothing on its own — the number is entirely a function of whether you matched on
exact email, fuzzy name, or address proximity, and the next person to run the audit
will get a different answer unless the rule travels with it.

### Stage 2: collection and integration

Once you know what you need, gather and consolidate it.

Be specific about the requirement. How much history do you need, and which attributes actually matter? On volume, there is no universal answer, but the
ordering is reliable: a simple classifier over a handful of well-separated classes
needs far less than a model predicting a rare outcome from many weak signals, and
training a deep model from scratch needs more again by a wide margin. What
actually governs it is how many examples you have of the *rarest class you care
about* — the total row count is the number people quote and the least informative
one. Establish the requirement empirically: train on a fraction of what you have,
then on more, and see whether performance is still climbing.

Combining sources brings two problems. The first is schema: the same concept under different field names (`customer_id` against `cust_num`), the same value in different types (dates as strings, dates as timestamps), and the same quantity in different units (dollars against cents, metres against feet). Define one master schema and the transformation rules into it:

```
Source A: customer_id (string) → Master: customer_identifier (integer)
Source B: cust_num (integer) → Master: customer_identifier (integer)
```

The second is time. Events land in different systems at different moments, time zones disagree, and batch capture sits alongside real-time capture. Pick one timestamp standard — UTC — and write down the synchronisation rules rather than leaving them in someone's head.

### Stage 3: cleaning

This is usually the longest phase.

**Missing values.** You have three options. Deletion removes records with missing critical values, and is right when the affected records are a small share of the total *and* the missingness is random. The second condition is the one that matters and the one nobody checks — if a field is missing because of something systematic, deleting those rows deletes a population. Imputation fills the gap with an estimate: mean or median for numbers, mode for categories, a model for anything more complicated. Flagging keeps the record and adds a "missing" indicator, letting the model learn that the absence itself means something, which is often true.

```
Is the field critical for prediction?
├─ Yes: Can you impute reliably?
│  ├─ Yes: Impute using appropriate method
│  └─ No: Delete record or flag for manual review
└─ No: Keep record, use missing indicator
```

**Duplicates.** They corrupt training by making some examples count more than once. Exact matches are easy:

```sql
DELETE FROM customers
WHERE id NOT IN (
  SELECT MIN(id)
  FROM customers
  GROUP BY email, name, address
)
```

Fuzzy duplicates are the real work: Levenshtein distance for text similarity, phonetic matching for names, geographic proximity for addresses. Whatever you pick, write it down — see the note on detection rules above.

**Errors.** They arrive in three shapes. Format errors, where the same phone number appears as (555) 123-4567, 5551234567 and 555-123-4567, and dates arrive in every ordering anyone has ever used. Out-of-range values: an age of 200 or -5, a historical event dated next year, a negative quantity where negatives are impossible. And inconsistent categories, where "NY", "New York", "NY State" and "new york" are four different places as far as the model is concerned.

Regular expressions handle the pattern-based fixes, lookup tables handle standardisation, and automated validation rules catch the rest — with a human path for the exceptions, because there will be exceptions.

### Stage 4: transformation

Convert the data into something a model can consume.

Categorical variables need encoding. One-hot encoding gives each category its own binary column:

```
Color: [Red, Blue, Green]
→ Color_Red: [1,0,0], Color_Blue: [0,1,0], Color_Green: [0,0,1]
```

Label encoding assigns numbers instead:

```
Size: [Small, Medium, Large] → [1, 2, 3]
```

Only use it where the order is real (Small &lt; Medium &lt; Large). Numbering unordered categories tells the model that category 3 sits between 2 and 4, which it does not.

Numerical variables usually need scaling, either normalised into a 0-1 range:

```
normalized = (value - min) / (max - min)
```

or standardised around the mean:

```
standardized = (value - mean) / standard_deviation
```

Then there is feature engineering, which is where domain knowledge earns its money. Dates become day of week, month, quarter, and time since the last event. Aggregates summarise a customer's total purchases, average order value and frequency, or a product's rating, review count and return rate. Interactions combine columns: price times quantity is revenue, clicks over impressions is a click-through rate. Text yields length metrics, sentiment scores, named entities, topics.

### Stage 5: validation and quality assurance

Before training on it, check it.

Look at the distributions. Are the numbers skewed? Are the categories balanced, or does one dominate? Are there outliers you cannot explain? An imbalance like this one will bend the model toward electronics and there is nothing in the training run that will tell you:

```
Product Categories:
- Electronics: 95,000 examples
- Clothing: 3,200 examples
- Food: 180 examples

This severe imbalance will cause AI to be biased toward Electronics.
```

Check the relationships too. Do the correlations make business sense? Is there a pattern nobody can account for? An unexplained correlation is either a discovery or a leak, and it is usually a leak.

Then write the business rules down as code, so they run every time rather than when someone remembers:

```python
# Example validation rules
assert all(df['age'] >= 0) and all(df['age'] <= 120), "Invalid ages detected"
assert all(df['order_date'] <= df['ship_date']), "Ship date before order date"
assert df['revenue'].sum() == (df['price'] * df['quantity']).sum(), "Revenue mismatch"
```

And report on it regularly:

```
Weekly Data Quality Report:

Sources Processed:  <count>
Total Records:      <count>
New Records:        <count since last report>

Quality Metrics (each with its change since last report):
- Completeness:     <populated fields / expected fields>
- Accuracy:         <records passing validation / records checked>
- Duplicates found: <count, by detection rule>
- Errors corrected: <count, by category>

Top Issues:
1. <issue>  <affected record count>
2. <issue>  <affected record count>
3. <issue>  <affected record count>
```

The change-since-last-week column is the part that earns its keep. An absolute
completeness figure tells you little; a completeness figure that dropped sharply
overnight tells you an upstream system changed, and that is the alert you actually
want.

## Common problems

### Not enough history

The symptom is a learning curve still climbing when you run out of data: more examples would clearly help, and you don't have them.

The options are to use a simpler approach that needs less data, augment what you have where the data type allows it, start from a pre-trained model instead of from scratch, buy or license more, or wait while you collect. Waiting is unpopular and is sometimes the right answer.

### Imbalanced data

Fraud detection is the standard example: legitimate transactions outnumber fraudulent ones by orders of magnitude, and a model that labels everything legitimate scores brilliantly on accuracy while being worthless.

You can oversample the minority class, undersample the majority, synthesise new minority examples with something like SMOTE, or weight the classes in the algorithm. But the first fix is the metric. If you are still reporting accuracy on a problem like this, nothing else you change will show up.

### Privacy and compliance

If the data is personal, the obligations arrive with it — GDPR, CCPA, HIPAA, depending on where you and it are.

Anonymisation strips identifying information. Pseudonymisation replaces identifiers with tokens. Differential privacy adds statistical noise. Synthetic data generates artificial records with the same shape. Federated learning trains without centralising anything. Each buys privacy at some cost in utility, and the trade-off is a decision for somebody with authority, not a technical default.

### Legacy systems

When the data that matters sits in a mainframe or a system nobody maintains, the routes out are: build a modern interface to it, schedule batch exports into somewhere newer, replicate it continuously, or migrate it in pieces. Whichever you choose, the extraction becomes a thing you own and maintain — plan for that rather than treating it as a one-off.

## Practices worth keeping

**Document the lineage.** Where each field came from, how it was extracted, what was done to it, which checks it passed, and what is known to be wrong with it. The last item is the one that saves someone six months from now.

**Version the data like code.**

```
customer_data_v1.0_2025-01-15.csv
customer_data_v1.1_2025-02-01.csv (added email validation)
customer_data_v2.0_2025-03-01.csv (schema change: split name field)
```

**Automate the repetitive parts** — extraction, the cleaning rules you have already decided, validation, error reporting. Anything done by hand every week will eventually be done wrong, or not at all.

**Start small and iterate.** Get a minimum viable dataset, train something, and let the model's failures tell you which data problems actually matter. Fix those, retrain, repeat. This beats a data-cleaning project with no model attached, which has no way of knowing when it is finished.

**Involve the people who know the business.** A data scientist can spot a statistical anomaly. Only someone who does the work can tell you whether it is normal variation or a broken sensor, whether those categories mean anything, and whether that relationship is expected or suspicious.

## Measuring whether the preparation worked

On process, track how much of the project went into data preparation, what share of records pass the quality checks, and how quickly errors get corrected.

On outcome, compare model accuracy on prepared against raw data, training time, and errors in production.

That comparison only means something if you keep the raw-data baseline.
Train once on the unprepared data first, however badly it performs, and preserve
that result. Teams routinely skip this because the raw run seems like wasted
effort, and then have no way to show what the preparation bought — or to
notice when a cleaning rule made things worse.

Be specific about which metric you are claiming. Faster training and higher accuracy are
different results with different causes: deduplication shrinks the dataset and
speeds up training without necessarily improving anything, and a rise in accuracy
after you dropped incomplete records may just mean you removed the hard cases.

## Conclusion

Data preparation is unglamorous, and it is where most of the difference gets decided between a model that works in production and one that only ever worked in the notebook.

Understand what you have before you go collecting more. Clean it properly, because poor data quality guarantees poor performance no matter what sits on top of it. Engineer the features with someone who knows the domain. Keep the quality checks running after the initial push, since data decays. And automate whatever you will have to do again, because manual preparation does not scale past the first month.

None of this is the interesting part of an AI project. It is the part that determines whether there is a project.
