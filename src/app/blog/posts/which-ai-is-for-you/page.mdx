Your project software has AI in it now. So does your accounting package, apparently. There's a fold-out in the trade magazine, three emails from suppliers you've never bought from, and a whole aisle of it at the show. Some of that reflects a real change in what software can do. A good share is a new name on a feature that shipped four years ago.

You don't have time to sort it out, and the people selling to you have no reason to help. I build this stuff, and I'd still rather you bought nothing than bought the wrong thing. Here's how I'd read the pitches.

## Distrust the numbers

You'll be shown figures on how many businesses use AI and how much time it saves. Most are worth nothing to you, for two reasons.

One is what gets counted. "Uses AI in at least one part of the business" counts a shop where someone once had a chatbot write a job ad the same as a shop that rebuilt its dispatch. The survey measures the first and gets quoted as proof of the second.

The other is who measured. Time-saved figures usually come from the people who picked the tool, on the tasks it's good at, without subtracting the time spent checking its output.

The alternative is to produce one number of your own. [Count one job for two weeks](/blog/count-it-first) before you change anything. That number will be more use to you than any statistic in a brochure.

## Distrust the urgency

"The window is closing" is one of the oldest lines in technology sales. Its job is to shorten your evaluation. A business that waits six months and picks well does not end up behind one that moved in six weeks and picked badly.

You aren't late.

## What has changed

Three things are different from a few years ago, and they matter more in the trades than in most places.

It handles messy input. Older software needed the clean version: the typed form, the caller who spoke clearly. Your input is a slip photographed at an angle in a cab, a voicemail with a compressor behind it, a note on the back of a card. Software that only worked on tidy input never did much for you. This is the change worth your attention.

Some of it now carries out a sequence. It reads the document, checks it against the order, flags the discrepancy, and puts it in front of a person. That's more useful and more dangerous, because a system that acts on something untrue acts on it fast. Give it a stale van stock list and it will order parts confidently and wrongly. For any of these, ask what it's allowed to do without asking, and who decided that.

Narrow usually beats general on your material. A system set up on your documents, your job numbers, and your customers' names will normally do better on your work than a general-purpose one, since it has less to guess. It's cheap to test. Try both on twenty of your own documents and you'll know in an afternoon.

## The hard part is the plumbing

If I could tell an owner one thing before they sign, it would be this.

Reading the delivery slip is the easy half. Getting those numbers into the system you already run, in the format it wants, without breaking whatever else writes to it, and in a way that survives the supplier changing their form, is where the hours go. The reading has gotten cheap. The plumbing hasn't.

So ask every vendor: what does it connect to, and who does that work? A vague answer means you're being quoted for half a job.

## Buy the standard thing when one exists

Most of what a small trade business needs can already be bought. A dispatch system. A decent job management package. An answering service with people on the phones. If a product covers most of what you need and the gap isn't what sets you apart from the shop down the road, buy it. It's cheaper, someone else maintains it, and it'll be around in five years.

Build only where you're unusual: the process nobody sells software for, the documents only your customers produce. That's a short list, and it should be. If the right answer on our call is "buy the off-the-shelf one," that's what I'll tell you.

Sometimes the answer isn't software. If your count shows the retyping takes twenty minutes a week, leave it alone.

## What gets skipped

Your crew will have the job-security conversation with or without you. Have it early and be specific about what changes for each of them.

Your records set the ceiling. Nothing built on them can be better than [what's in them](/blog/messy-records).

And anything you connect to your data is a new door into your business. Someone should decide to open it on purpose.

## Two questions

"How fast can we adopt AI" has no useful answer. It's the question the sales material wants you asking.

Ask these two: which single job in your week would you point this at first, and how would you know afterward whether it worked? If you can't answer the second, hold off on the first and go count.
