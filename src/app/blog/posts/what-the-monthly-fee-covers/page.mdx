My pricing has two numbers on it: one to build the thing and one every month after. The second number gets questions, and it should. Plenty of monthly fees in this business pay for nothing. So here's what mine pays for, and how you'd check that anyone's does.

## Why a finished build doesn't stay finished

A furnace, once installed, sits in the same basement burning the same gas. An automation sits between other people's systems, and those keep changing.

The paper changes. Your lumber supplier redesigns their delivery ticket and the PO number moves from the top right to a box near the bottom. A new customer sends work orders in a format nothing has seen before. The scanner at the front desk gets replaced with one that produces darker images.

The connections change. The software you're feeding (job management, accounting, dispatch) ships an update and a field gets renamed. A login method gets retired. The phone carrier changes how voicemail audio is delivered.

The model changes. The AI model doing the reading gets replaced by a newer version every so often. Newer is usually better overall and slightly different on your particular odd cases. The handwriting it used to read one way, it now reads another.

Your business changes. You add a service line. You start working in a town whose street names nothing has heard before. Your idea of "urgent" shifts when you hire a second crew.

None of these sends an alert. What you'd see, if you were looking, is the share of items it reads correctly slipping a little each week. If nobody's looking, you find out when a customer calls.

## What watching it involves

In practice it's a few specific habits.

A weekly sample. Pull a handful of real items the system processed and check them by hand against the source. If it read twelve slips right last month and ten this month, something moved.

A count compared to last week. How many items came through, how many it flagged as unsure, how many a person corrected. The level matters less than the change. A jump in "unsure" flags after a quiet month usually means a form changed.

Fixing what moved. Adjusting for the new delivery ticket. Updating the connection when the other software changes. Re-testing against your saved ugly examples whenever the underlying model is updated, before the new version touches live work.

Small adjustments you ask for. "Stop treating thermostat questions as urgent." "Add the new warehouse." Each one is small, and without them people slowly stop trusting the output.

Keeping the off switch working. The fallback to the old way (plain voicemail, the paper tray) should still work, and someone on your side should still know how to use it.

## How to tell a real fee from a fake one

Ask any vendor, me included, four questions.

What did you check last month? There should be a specific answer: these samples, this count, this change.

What broke in the last six months, and how did you find out? "Nothing" is a bad answer for anything that's been running a while. Either it isn't being watched or it isn't being used.

What happens if I stop paying? You should hear exactly what keeps running, what stops, and what you'd own.

Can I run it myself? Sometimes yes. If you have someone in the office who's comfortable with this kind of thing, I'll price a build as handover-only and show them what to watch. Say so when we scope it.

## When there shouldn't be a monthly fee

An assessment is a one-time piece of work. You get a written report and nothing recurs. A one-workflow audit is the same.

A website is close to that. Mine have an optional yearly upkeep charge, and the hosting and domain stay in your name either way.

The monthly fee belongs on things that read, decide, or connect on your behalf every day. Those are the things that drift.
