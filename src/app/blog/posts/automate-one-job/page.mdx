The delivery came in two pallets short. The driver's slip said so, in pen, in the corner. The slip went in the tray, somebody typed it into the system on Thursday without reading the corner, and you found out eight days later when the customer called.

You probably have a version of this. The submittal that gets retyped into the project system by the one person who should have been on site. The after-hours call that went to voicemail and then to a competitor. You know which job it is. You may not know how often it happens.

What follows is the order I'd work through it. It's a sequence, and the useful thing about a sequence is that you can stop partway. Stopping after step two because the numbers said "don't bother" is a good result. The version that goes wrong skips the first two steps and starts with a demo somebody liked at a trade show.

## 1. Name one job in one sentence

"We should look at AI" isn't a job. Describe it the way you'd describe it to your foreman.

"Every delivery slip gets typed into the system by hand, twice if the driver's handwriting is bad."

"Calls after five go to voicemail and I lose the ones I don't return by seven."

"Field notes come back on paper and somebody matches them to job numbers on Friday afternoon."

If you can't get it into one sentence, you probably have two or three problems bundled together. Split them and pick the one that costs you the most sleep.

## 2. Count it before you change anything

Two weeks, on paper if that's what you have. How many slips a week, how long one takes, who does it, and what they weren't doing meanwhile.

Do this before anything gets installed. The "before" number stops existing the day the process changes, and every later argument about whether it was worth the money comes back to it. I wrote up [how to do the count](/blog/count-it-first) separately, including the ways it fools you.

Sometimes the count ends the project. That's fine. It was two weeks and a clipboard.

## 3. Build the smallest version that helps this month

The temptation is to do all of it: every document, every exception, wired into the system of record. That takes months, which is long enough for everyone to stop caring.

The smallest version of the paperwork job: slips get read, the numbers land in a sheet, and a person checks the sheet. Nobody's system got replaced and nothing got deleted.

The smallest version of the after-hours job: callers leave a message like always, and in the morning there's a written summary sorted into "no heat, no hot water" and "everything else." Nothing is booked or promised.

Small versions survive because their mistakes are cheap and visible. You catch them Tuesday morning with a coffee, before a customer does.

## 4. Run it beside the old way

Until you trust it, both run. The paper slips still go in the tray. The voicemail box still records.

That's a few weeks of mild duplication, and it buys you the same inputs handled both ways. You'll see where the new thing is better and where it's worse. Expect both.

## 5. Decide what happens when it's wrong

It will be wrong sometimes. Settle four things beforehand.

What does it do when it isn't sure? It should say so and hand the item to a person. A system that guesses confidently is worse than none, because you stop checking.

What happens when it's down? The internet, the phone line, or a service it depends on will be out on a day that matters. There has to be a way back to how you did it before, and someone on your side has to know it.

How does a person take over, and do they know it's their turn?

What does it keep a record of? In six weeks you'll want to know why something got sorted the way it did.

I go further into these in [who is answerable when it gets it wrong](/blog/who-is-answerable).

## 6. Try it on the ugly ones

Every demo uses the clean example: the typed slip, the calm caller in a quiet room.

Your week has the slip photographed at an angle in the cab with a thumb over the corner. The voicemail with a compressor running. The PDF that's really a photo of a fax.

Pull twenty of your worst real ones and run those. If it's going to fail, you want it to fail now, while this is still a conversation. There's more on this in [what your records need to look like](/blog/messy-records).

## 7. Give it to the person who'll complain

Skip the enthusiast. Pick the one who'll tell you it's annoying.

Give them two weeks, then ask what they've stopped using it for. That's a different question from what they think of it. People are polite about software and specific about their workarounds. If they went back to reading every slip because the sheet missed one once, you need to hear that.

At this stage the wording usually needs more work than the technology. Your "urgent" won't match what the person answering the phone means by urgent.

## 8. Turn the old way off one lane at a time

After-hours before daytime. One yard before four. One customer's paperwork before all of them.

Leave weeks between steps. Each one is a chance to stop, and the next crew hears it works from a coworker instead of from you.

## 9. Plan for it not staying fixed

An automation isn't a water heater you install and forget. Your supplier changes the layout of their delivery note. The software behind it gets updated and behaves a little differently on odd cases. A new customer's paperwork looks like nothing you've seen. The thing just gets quietly worse until someone notices it's been wrong for a month.

So someone has to watch it, and you should know who before you switch it on. That's [what the monthly fee is for](/blog/what-the-monthly-fee-covers) after a build.

## Where it goes off the rails

Your crew thinks it's about their jobs. They're already discussing it without you, so say something early. In this kind of work the honest answer is usually that it takes the retyping and leaves the trade. Say what changes for each person, and bring the one who does the job into the design. They know where the exceptions are.

The scope grows sideways. Once one thing works, everything looks automatable. Keep a list of what's next and let it stay a list for a while.

The connection is harder than the reading. Pulling numbers off a slip is the easy half. Getting them into the system you already run, in the format it wants, without breaking anything else that writes to it, takes most of the hours. A quote that only covers the reading covers half the job.

## How you'll know it worked

Early on you can only see whether people use it or work around it, whether it stays up, and how often it's right. None of that is money, but it tells you whether to continue.

A few months in, the numbers you counted in step two start to move: hours, jobs you'd have lost, shortages caught the same day.

Some of it never becomes a number. Ask yourself whether you'd object if I switched it off tomorrow.
