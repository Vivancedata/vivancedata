# Measuring AI ROI: Building a Compelling Business Case

Securing budget for an AI project takes more than enthusiasm. Whoever signs it off wants quantified returns and realistic timelines, and that is exactly where these cases tend to fall over, because the numbers are easy to produce and hard to justify.

This is not a set of returns you can expect. Nobody can give you that honestly: the return depends on your cost base, your volumes, your data, and how much of your process the tool actually displaces. What follows is how to build a case from your own numbers, which costs get left out of nearly every one, and how to attack the result before someone else does.

## Where AI business cases fall over

Projects lose their funding, or get cancelled halfway through, for a small number of repeating reasons.

The value proposition is vague. "AI will make us more efficient" does not justify a capital request of any size, and everyone in the room knows it.

The costs are half-counted. Software licences are visible and get counted; integration, training and maintenance are not and don't.

The timeline is optimistic in a way the author would not defend if asked. Weeks are promised where the work takes months.

And there is no measurement plan, so nobody can say afterwards whether the thing delivered anything at all.

## A framework for AI ROI analysis

### Step 0: baseline before you start

This step comes first and is the one most often skipped. Whatever you intend to
improve, measure it now, before anything is deployed — and measure it for long
enough to see its normal variation.

Record, for the specific process in scope: volume broken down by type rather than
as one total; time per unit, measured rather than estimated by the people who do
the work; fully loaded cost per unit, including the supervision and rework the
process needs; the error and rework rate, and what an error currently costs to
fix; and the seasonal pattern across at least a full cycle.

Without this, you cannot distinguish improvement from normal fluctuation, and
neither can anyone reviewing your results. A project that deploys in a quiet
quarter and reports the drop in volume as a benefit is not lying deliberately —
it simply has no way of knowing better. The baseline is the only thing that makes
the post-implementation number mean anything, and it is unrecoverable once the
system is live.

### Step 1: identify tangible benefits

Start with benefits you can measure and put a currency figure against.

Direct cost reduction is the most defensible: labour time saved at an hourly cost, errors avoided at a cost per error, transactions handled at a cost per transaction, waste reduced at a cost per unit.

Revenue effects are harder and need a stated mechanism — a conversion improvement against an average deal size, churn reduction against customer lifetime value, better pricing against transaction volume, or new customers at an average value. Each of those is a chain of assumptions, and each link is somewhere the case can break.

Risk reduction is the hardest of the three to bank, because you are counting things that did not happen: fines avoided, fraud losses prevented, defect costs sidestepped, breaches that didn't occur. Include them, but never let the case depend on them.

### Step 2: calculate total cost of ownership

Don't just count software licences. Take everything over a three-year horizon.

The direct costs are the easy ones: licensing or subscription fees, hardware and infrastructure, third-party services and APIs, and any implementation partner.

The internal costs are real money even though nobody invoices for them: your own team's time, training, change management, and ongoing maintenance and support.

Then there are the costs nobody itemises. Data preparation and cleaning, which routinely takes more effort than building the model. Integration development and testing. Redesigning the process around the new tool. And the opportunity cost of what the team would otherwise have been doing.

#### The costs that get forgotten

Three categories are missing from most AI business cases, and together they are
usually larger than the software line item.

*Integration.* The model is a component; the work is connecting it to the systems
that hold your data and the systems that consume its output. This is normal
software engineering, it is estimated like normal software engineering, and it is
the line most often filled in with a guess because it does not appear on the
vendor's quote.

*Change management.* People have to alter how they work, which means training,
a period of reduced throughput while they adjust, and the effort of handling
resistance. If your case assumes day-one productivity at the new steady state,
it is wrong by however long the adjustment takes.

*Ongoing upkeep.* AI systems are not build-once assets. Models drift as the world
they were trained on changes, prompts and rules need revision, upstream data
sources change format, and someone must watch the quality continuously. Budget for
this as a standing operational cost, not a warranty period. A business case that
shows maintenance falling to near zero after year one is describing a system
nobody is watching.

### Step 3: build your financial model

A spreadsheet with three periods will do.

Year 0 is implementation: all the upfront costs, little or no benefit, negative cash flow. Year 1 is stabilisation: ongoing subscription costs against partial benefits — assume a ramp rather than a step change, and state the ramp you assumed. Years 2 and 3 are steady state, where the costs settle and the benefits, if the case is right, arrive in full.

From that, four figures. The payback period: how long until cumulative benefits exceed costs. Net present value, in today's money. Internal rate of return, as an effective annual return. And ROI as a percentage — total benefits less total costs, over total costs.

## Pressure-testing your own model

You will find no worked examples with figures in this post, deliberately. Published
ROI examples are almost always reverse-engineered from a conclusion someone already
wanted, and borrowing their assumptions imports their errors into your case. What
follows is how to attack your own model before a finance director does.

### Attack the benefit side first

Take the largest benefit line in your model and ask four questions of it.

**Is the saved unit real, and does it convert to money?** A saving of some minutes
per transaction, multiplied by transaction volume, produces an impressive annual
hours figure. Those hours only become money if you actually reduce headcount, avoid
a hire you would otherwise have made, or redeploy the time onto work that generates
revenue. If none of those happen, the hours are absorbed and the saving is real for
the individual and invisible in the accounts. Say which of the three you are
claiming, in the case document.

**Would this have happened anyway?** Some of the improvement you will measure comes
from work that had nothing to do with the AI — a process someone tidied up, a
seasonal upswing, a policy change, staff who got better at the job. The tool tends
to be credited with all of it, because it is the visible intervention.

**Are you counting the same benefit twice?** Faster handling and reduced headcount
are frequently added together when one causes the other. So are error reduction and
rework cost avoidance.

**What is the residual?** Automation rarely removes a task; it removes the easy
instances and leaves the hard ones. If the routine cases go and the difficult cases
remain, average handling time on what is left goes *up*, and the cost per remaining
case may rise enough to eat much of the saving. Model the work you are left with,
not just the work you removed.

### Metrics that move too easily

Some numbers can be improved without anything improving. Be suspicious of these,
particularly when they are the ones being reported.

Deflection or containment rate treats an interaction abandoned in frustration and
one genuinely resolved as the same event. Pair it with a satisfaction or
repeat-contact measure on the deflected traffic specifically, or it tells you
nothing.

Average handling time falls when the tool works. It also falls when staff are
rushing, or when the hard cases got routed somewhere else.

Ticket or case volume falls when problems are solved, and also when reporting a
problem becomes harder.

Accuracy on an aggregate test set rises when the model improves, and also when the
test set is easier than production.

Adoption and usage counts measure that people opened the tool, not that it helped
them. Mandated usage produces excellent adoption numbers.

The general rule: a metric that a system's owner can move by changing their own
behaviour will eventually be moved that way, without anyone intending to cheat.
For each headline metric in your case, name the second metric that would catch it
being gamed, and commit to reporting both.

### Sensitivity analysis is the actual deliverable

Single-point estimates invite an argument about whether your number is right.
Ranges move the conversation to the assumptions, which is where it belongs.

For each major assumption — adoption rate, automation rate, time saved per unit,
ramp duration — model a pessimistic, expected, and optimistic case. Then find the
break-even: how far can each assumption fall before the project stops paying for
itself? That single figure is more persuasive than any ROI percentage, because it
tells a decision-maker exactly what they are betting on. If the case only works at
the optimistic end of every assumption simultaneously, you have learned something
important before spending anything.

## Building the business case document

### Executive summary (1 page)

Present the essence in a format a decision-maker can absorb in two minutes. Four
blocks, each filled from your own baseline rather than from anyone's benchmark.

*The problem.* The current volume, the measured cost per unit, and the share that
is routine — each traceable to the baseline you took in Step 0. Name what that
resource is being kept from doing.

*The solution.* What the system will handle, and explicitly what it will not. The
scope boundary is the most useful sentence in this section, because it is the one
you will be held to.

*The investment.* Total cost over the horizon, split into upfront and ongoing,
with integration, change management and upkeep as visible lines rather than folded
into a single implementation figure.

*The return.* The expected benefit as a range, the assumption it is most sensitive
to, and the break-even point on that assumption. A single confident number reads as
precision and invites the reader to test it; a range with a stated break-even reads
as competence and invites them to discuss the assumption.

### Problem statement (1-2 pages)

Quantify the current state, explain what it costs the business, say why now rather than next year, and be honest about what happens if nothing changes — which is sometimes "not much", and is worth knowing.

### Proposed solution (2-3 pages)

What the system is, how it works in language a non-technical reader follows, how it would be implemented, the timeline with its milestones, and the measures you will report against.

### Financial analysis (2-3 pages)

The cost breakdown, the benefit calculations with every assumption written next to the number it produced, the three-year model, the sensitivity ranges, and the headline metrics.

### Risk assessment (1 page)

Technical risks, organisational risks, market risks, and what you would do about each. Include the contingency plan, and include the condition under which you would stop.

### Appendices

Detailed assumptions, vendor comparisons, reference cases you have actually spoken to, and technical specifications.

## Common pitfalls

### 1. Overpromising results

Wrong: a confident reduction figure with no stated origin.

Right: a projected reduction on a named subset of the work, with the source of the
projection stated — your own pilot, ideally — and the conditions under which it
would not hold.

### 2. Ignoring change management costs

The mistake is budgeting for software and implementation only. Adoption, training
and process change are a substantial cost in their own right, and unlike licence
fees they land on people who already have other jobs to do.

### 3. Underestimating the timeline

The mistake is promising delivery on the vendor's implementation timeline. That
timeline covers configuring the product. It does not cover getting your data into
usable shape, integrating with your systems, security and procurement review, or
the period after go-live when people are still learning. Estimate those from your
own experience of comparable projects, because your organization's procurement and
integration speed is the binding constraint, not the technology.

### 4. Focusing only on hard costs

A labour saving quoted on its own is incomplete. State the service-quality and
speed effects too, as separately measured outcomes, clearly marked as secondary
and not silently converted into currency to inflate the headline.

### 5. Single-point estimates

A single exact savings figure is risky, because precision the underlying
assumptions cannot support reads as false confidence to anyone who has sat through
a project before. Give a range and name what drives it — usually adoption rate or
automation rate — so the reviewer can argue with the assumption rather than the
arithmetic.

## Making the case stand up

### Compare against your own track record

The most credible comparison is internal: how this project's projected return and
payback sit against the IT and process projects your organization has actually
funded, and how those turned out against their own business cases. Your finance
team has that history. Using it puts the AI project on the same footing as
everything else competing for the money, which is where a sound case wants to be.

### Handle industry benchmarks carefully

Analyst and vendor figures are the weakest evidence in any AI business case, and
reviewers know it. They are typically averaged across organizations unlike yours,
frequently sourced from the vendors selling the tools, and rarely accompanied by
the failures. If you cite one, cite it as context rather than as the basis of your
projection, name the source precisely, and never present a benchmark as
"conservative" — you have no way to know that.

A pilot on your own data is worth more than every published benchmark combined,
and is usually cheaper than the argument you will otherwise have.

### Show what will be visible early

Identify what becomes measurable soon and say what it will and will not prove.
Early indicators tend to be leading rather than financial — the system is in use,
its outputs are accurate on your real inputs — and presenting those honestly as
evidence that the project is on track beats claiming savings that could not yet
have accumulated.

### Address skepticism directly

Acknowledge that AI projects fail, and say what specifically reduces that risk
here: a scoped pilot with a defined kill criterion, budgeted training, a reference
you have actually spoken to. State the kill criterion in the case document. A
project with a pre-agreed condition for stopping is far easier to approve than one
that can only ever be declared a success.

### Connect it to something the business already cares about

Tie the project to a goal that existed before the project did. If you cannot name
one, that is worth pausing on: a case that has to invent its own strategic
justification is usually solving a problem nobody was asking about.

## Measuring success after implementation

Once it is approved and live, report the measures you promised.

In the first three months you can see adoption, uptime, and early accuracy on real inputs. Between months four and nine, process efficiency, cost movement and user satisfaction start to mean something. From month ten onwards you can compare full impact against the business case and say what you learned.

Build a dashboard people will actually look at, showing predicted against actual benefits with the original prediction left visible and unedited; cost tracking, including the internal time nobody invoiced for; each headline metric next to the counter-metric that would catch it being gamed; and adoption.

### Report against the original case, not a revised one

The single most useful discipline here is refusing to update the projection. Once
a business case is approved, the numbers in it become the standard you are measured
against; quietly restating them as reality arrives converts the exercise into
narration. Keep the approved version, report actuals beside it, and explain the
variance in both directions.

Variance in your favour is as informative as variance against you. A benefit that
substantially exceeds the projection usually means an assumption was wrong rather
than that the project overachieved, and finding out which is what makes your next
business case more accurate. Organizations that get good at this are not the ones
whose projects always hit their numbers — they are the ones that know why their
projects miss.

## Conclusion

A strong business case turns an interesting idea into a funded one. There is a
second reason to build one properly, though, and it is the better reason: to find
out, before you spend the money, whether the project is worth doing at all.

The short version: baseline first, because the measurement you skip before
deployment cannot be recovered afterwards. Cost the invisible parts — integration,
change management, upkeep — as line items. Document every assumption, so reviewers
argue with assumptions rather than totals. Give ranges instead of points, and name
the break-even on the assumption that matters most. Pick metrics that resist
gaming, or pair each with the counter-metric that catches it. And report against
the original case, including when the variance is in your favour.

A case built this way is harder to write and considerably harder to argue with.
It also has a property the confident version does not: if the project is not
actually worth doing, this process tells you so while it is still cheap to find
out.
