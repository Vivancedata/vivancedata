The customer who rings at 9pm with no heat does not know they are talking to something you bought. They think they are talking to your company, because as far as they are concerned they are. Whatever it says, you said.

That is the whole of what people mean by AI ethics once you are actually running one of these things. Not a policy document. Five questions, each with an answer you can write on one page before you switch it on, and each of which gets settled either deliberately now or badly later by whoever happens to be under the most pressure.

## 1. It has to be willing to say it does not know

This is the one I would put above all the others, and it is the one that separates a system worth having from one that will cost you a customer.

Anything reading a scrawled note or listening to a voicemail will sometimes not be able to tell. The house number was said once, fast, over a running compressor. The job number on the slip has a character that could be a 1 or a letter. The correct behaviour there is to stop and hand it to a person, marked as unclear. The wrong behaviour is a confident guess, because a confident guess is indistinguishable from a correct answer until somebody drives to the wrong address.

So ask, of anything you are shown: what does it do when it is not sure, and can I see that happen? A demonstration that never shows you a case it declines is showing you the easy half. Systems that flag their own uncertainty are less impressive in the room and considerably better on a Tuesday, because you learn where the edge is instead of finding it by accident.

## 2. You have to be able to say why it did what it did

A customer rings up annoyed because they were told Thursday and they expected Tuesday. Or a call that should have gone through as an emergency got queued as a routine service request. Someone in your office has to be able to look at that and say what happened.

"The system decided" is not an answer you can give a customer, and it is not an answer you should accept from a supplier either. What you want is plain: the recording or the document it worked from, what it pulled out of it, what it did next, and when. Not model documentation — a line a person can read.

Ask before you buy whether you can see that trail, how far back it goes, and whether you can get at it yourself without ringing anybody. The day you need it is never a day you have time to wait.

## 3. It will not hear everyone equally well

Voice systems do better with some speakers than others. A strong accent, a bad line from a rural property, a hands-free set in a van, an older customer who takes a while to get to the point, a jobsite roaring behind whoever is calling. Document readers have the same shape of problem: they do worse on handwriting than print, and worse on a photograph than a scan.

The commercial risk here is not abstract. The callers it serves worst are the least likely to complain about it. They will not write to you about the transcription. They will ring the next number on the list, and you will never learn that it happened.

The check is not complicated. Take a set of real calls that includes the difficult ones — the accents you actually get, the noisy sites, the old handsets — and look at how it does on those specifically rather than on the average. Then do it again in six months, because these things drift as what feeds them changes.

## 4. You are now holding things you were not holding before

Put a system on the phone line and you are storing recordings of customers. Put one on your field paperwork and you are storing photographs taken inside people's houses, gate codes, alarm details, the note that says the key is under the pot, and occasionally a card number that somebody read out onto a voicemail because they were trying to be helpful.

You were arguably holding some of that before, in a voicemail box and a filing cabinet. The difference is that it is now in a system, probably somebody else's, and there is more of it.

Three questions cover most of it. What is being kept, and for how long? Where does it sit, and who else can see it? And if a customer asks you to delete everything you hold about them, could you? Work that out while it is a calm conversation. It is a short one at your scale, and it is a bad one to have for the first time in front of an angry customer.

## 5. Decide now what it may do without asking

There is a line between a system that writes things down for you and a system that acts. Reading a voicemail and putting a summary on your phone commits you to nothing. Booking a slot, promising an arrival window, ordering a part, telling a customer their delivery is on the way — each of those spends your money or your reputation.

Draw the line explicitly and write it down, because an undrawn line gets drawn by whoever is busiest. My own preference is to start with everything on the writing-down side and move one thing across at a time, once you have watched it be right for a few weeks on that specific thing.

Whoever signs off needs to be able to do it properly. If somebody is approving forty items in a morning with a customer on hold, they are not a check, they are a formality, and everyone involved knows it. And there needs to be a way to turn it off — not an escalation, not a support ticket, a switch, and somebody other than you who knows where it is.

## Your name is on the van

When it gets something wrong, the customer does not blame a vendor they have never heard of. They blame you, and they are right to, because you chose it.

That is not an argument against using any of this. It is an argument for knowing, before you switch it on, which decisions it is making on your behalf, what it does when it is unsure, and who picks up the phone when it gets one wrong. Settling that costs you an afternoon. Not settling it costs you the customer whose frozen pipe went into the wrong queue at 9pm, and everybody they tell.
