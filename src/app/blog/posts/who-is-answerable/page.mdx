The customer who calls at 9pm with no heat doesn't know they're talking to something you bought. As far as they're concerned they're talking to your company. Whatever it says, you said.

That's most of what "AI ethics" comes to once you're running one of these. You don't need a policy document. You need answers to five questions, and you can write them on one page before you switch anything on. Leave them open and they get settled later by whoever is under the most pressure that day.

## 1. It has to be willing to say it doesn't know

I'd put this first. It's the difference between a system worth having and one that costs you a customer.

Anything reading a scrawled note or listening to a voicemail will sometimes be unable to tell. The house number was said once, fast, over a running compressor. The job number on the slip has a character that could be a 1 or a letter. The right behavior is to stop and hand it to a person, marked unclear. A confident guess looks exactly like a correct answer until somebody drives to the wrong address.

So ask of anything you're shown: what does it do when it isn't sure, and can I watch that happen? A demo that never declines a case is showing you the easy half. Systems that flag their own uncertainty look less impressive in the sales meeting and work better on a Tuesday, because you find out where the edge is.

## 2. You have to be able to say why it did what it did

A customer calls annoyed because they were told Thursday and expected Tuesday. Or a call that should have been an emergency got queued as routine. Someone in your office has to be able to look that up and say what happened.

"The system decided" won't satisfy a customer, and you shouldn't accept it from a vendor. You want the recording or document it worked from, what it pulled out, what it did next, and when, in a form a person can read.

Before you buy, ask whether you can see that trail, how far back it goes, and whether you can get to it without calling anyone. You'll need it on a day when you can't wait.

## 3. It won't hear everyone equally well

Voice systems do better with some speakers than others. A strong accent, a bad line from a rural property, a speakerphone in a van, an older customer who takes a while to get to the point, a jobsite roaring in the background. Document readers have the same weakness: worse on handwriting than print, worse on a photo than a scan.

This costs you business quietly. The callers it serves worst are the least likely to complain. They call the next number on the list and you never hear about it.

To check, pull a set of real calls that includes the hard ones (the accents you get, the noisy sites, the old handsets) and look at how it does on those specifically. Do it again in six months, because performance drifts as the inputs change.

## 4. You're now holding things you weren't before

Put a system on the phone line and you're storing recordings of customers. Put one on field paperwork and you're storing photos taken inside people's houses, gate codes, alarm details, the note about the key under the pot, and now and then a card number somebody read onto a voicemail trying to be helpful.

You held some of that before, in a voicemail box and a filing cabinet. Now it's in a system, probably someone else's, and there's more of it.

Three questions cover most of it. What is kept, and for how long? Where does it sit, and who else can see it? If a customer asked you to delete everything you hold on them, could you? Work that out while it's a calm conversation.

## 5. Decide what it may do without asking

A system that writes things down for you commits you to nothing. A system that acts spends your money or your reputation: booking a slot, promising an arrival window, ordering a part, telling a customer their delivery is on the way.

Write down where that line sits. If you don't, whoever is busiest will draw it for you. I'd start with everything on the writing-down side and move one thing across at a time, after you've watched it be right on that specific thing for a few weeks.

Whoever signs off needs room to do it properly. Someone approving forty items in a morning with a customer on hold is a formality, and everyone knows it. There also has to be an off switch: a real one that works without filing a support ticket, and someone besides you who knows where it is.

## Your name is on the van

When it gets something wrong, the customer doesn't blame a vendor they've never heard of. They blame you, and that's fair, because you chose it.

You can still use these systems. Just know, before you switch one on, which decisions it makes for you, what it does when it's unsure, and who picks up when it gets one wrong. Settling that takes an afternoon.
